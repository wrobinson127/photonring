How it works

Nothing here is a picture or a video. Your graphics card draws every frame from the equations below.

1. Light paths

For each pixel, one ray is traced backward from the camera. Around a non-spinning black hole (the Schwarzschild solution), a light path obeys

d²u/dφ² + u = (3/2) rs u²,   u = 1/r

which is the same path a particle takes under the force below, so each ray is stepped with a leapfrog integrator, up to 220 steps per pixel:

a = −(3/2) rs h² r̂ / r⁴,   h = |r × v|

2. The disk

Gas orbits in a thin disk from the innermost stable orbit at 3 rs, where it moves at half the speed of light past anyone hovering there. Its color is a black body at the temperature the camera would measure after the exact frequency shift

g = √(1 − 3rs/2r) / (1 − Ωℓ)
Ω = √(rs/2r³)

and its brightness scales as g⁴. The side turning toward you is hotter and brighter. The side turning away is redder and dim.

3. What you are seeing

The arc over the top of the shadow is the far side of the disk. Its light was bent up and over the hole. The thin bright ring hugging the shadow is light that circled the hole before escaping.

5. Things you launch

Click to launch a meteor, a planet, a star or a rocket. Hold, then drag, to choose its direction and speed. The blue line is the path it will take. Each one free-falls along the path general relativity gives a body near the hole, stepped in its own time:

a = −(rs/2) r̂/r² − (3/2) rs h² r̂/r⁴

The second term is what Newton lacks. It makes orbits swing around instead of closing, and it pulls in anything that comes too near. Seen from far away, a falling object's clock slows as it nears the horizon, so it reddens and fades instead of crossing.

6. Drag and tides

An orbit does not decay by itself. Things fall in here because the disk slows them. Inside the disk an object is dragged toward the speed of the gas, which circles the hole and drifts inward. Where it ploughs through, the heated gas glows.

Gravity pulls harder on the near side of an object than on the far side. Close in, that difference beats the gravity holding a planet or a star together. The page then swaps it for a few hundred pieces that keep its velocity but start at different points across it, so each piece follows its own orbit and they spread into a stream. A meteor or a rocket is held together by the strength of its material, and stays whole.

Each piece is drawn where its light reaches you. For every piece, the graphics card searches for the bent light path from the camera to it, the short way round the hole and the long way round. That is why debris behind the hole shows up twice.

7. Riding along

Ride along puts the camera on the path of the last thing you launched, or on a path of its own if nothing is flying. A moving camera sees two more effects. Light from the direction of travel arrives bunched together and shifted toward blue. Light from behind is spread out and reddened. Brightness follows the fourth power of that shift, which is why the ring blazes on the way in.

The camera crosses the horizon in a finite time on its own clock, while the far-away clock runs off without limit. Inside, light from outside still arrives, so the sky stays in view beside the shadow until the ride ends near the centre.

For a camera falling straight in from rest, general relativity puts the shadow's edge 42.1° from the middle of the hole at the moment of crossing. Tested against that exact curve, the render agrees within about 1% from 20 rs down to 0.25 rs. The Prediction button draws the exact edge for whatever path you are riding.

What is not physics

The streaks in the disk are procedural noise, drawn for texture. The sky is invented too: its stars, star band, gas clouds and galaxies are generated by the page and are not a map of the real sky. The disk's temperature scale and the exposure are set for the eye, and its temperature falls off by the standard Newtonian thin-disk formula. The hole does not spin and the disk has no thickness. Outside a ride, the camera hovers in place. Everything is drawn as if its light arrived at once, so the delay light takes to travel is left out.

Launched objects are drawn far too large for the hole. The distances at which the planet and the star come apart are picked to be seen, not computed: where tides really win depends on the hole's mass and the object's density. The disk's drag is set far stronger than a real disk's, so that things fall in while you watch. Debris does not collide or join the disk, and it fades after a while. The brightness of its second image is only roughly corrected for lensing. The rocket only coasts. The looks and lighting of objects are decoration. Their colour is not shifted by their own motion, or by the camera's on a ride.

On a ride, playback slows as you fall so the last part can be watched, the exposure closes down when the view fills with bright gas, and the rest of the scene never runs more than three times faster than your own clock. The camera is not torn apart, and the ride stops at 0.16 rs, short of the centre. Directions that receive no light from outside are drawn black.
